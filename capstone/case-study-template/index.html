Project Overview

Idol Chase is a point-and-click narrative game that follows the story of a young girl and her deepening parasocial relationship with her celebrity crush. In a bid to increase profits, the celebrity’s management company launches an AI chatbot designed to mimic the star’s personality and chat with fans. What begins as innocent interaction soon spirals into obsession, blurring the lines between reality and simulation, and ultimately endangering both the fans and the idol.

The game is intended for audiences familiar with idol culture, celebrity fandoms, or the psychological effects of one-sided relationships. It acts as a cautionary tale about the unchecked use of artificial intelligence in spaces driven by profit, where emotional manipulation is monetized and human connection is sacrificed in favor of curated fantasy.

Through stylized visuals, immersive storytelling, and interactive mini-games, Idol Chase encourages players to question the ethics behind commodifying intimacy and the consequences of replacing real connection with artificial simulation. It offers not just entertainment, but a social critique—inviting reflection on how far companies will go to blur emotional boundaries in the name of engagement and revenue.

Project Research

In January of this year, news broke that Character Technologies, the company behind the popular chatbot Character.AI, was being sued by the parents of two children in Texas. The lawsuit alleged that the chatbot encouraged self-harm, violence, and sexual behavior among their children. Prior to this controversy, Character.AI had gained widespread popularity for its ability to simulate conversations with virtually any persona, from fictional characters to real-life celebrities.

This incident highlights a critical oversight in the development and regulation of such technologies. The potential dangers should have been anticipated, especially given the long-standing intensity of celebrity and idol culture. In many Eastern entertainment industries, idols are required to maintain a carefully curated image: hiding romantic relationships, enduring stalkers, and even adopting fake birthdays to align with marketable star signs.

These manufactured personas are designed to deepen fan attachment, but often at the cost of the idol's personal identity and safety. As we continue to blur the lines between reality and simulation, we must ask: when does fan engagement cross into exploitation? And at what point are we commodifying someone's existence for profit?

Visual Design

The visual design of Idol Chase was inspired by the desire to reflect the aesthetic typically associated with obsessive idol fandoms: soft, sweet, and emotionally immersive. I aimed to create a cutesy, anime-esque art style, complete with oversized, glistening eyes and rounded, approachable character designs that mirror the visual language often seen in fan-made media and merchandise.

The color palette plays a crucial role in setting the tone. I chose light, pastel shades, mainly soft pinks and baby blues, to create a sense of innocence and comfort. This deliberately charming atmosphere is an attempt to disarm the player, drawing them into a world that feels safe and familiar. As the narrative progresses and darker themes begin to emerge, the aesthetic contrast hopefully grows unsettling, reinforcing the disconnect between surface-level fantasy and underlying reality.

User Interface

Idol Chase features four distinct gameplay types, each designed to serve a narrative purpose while gradually shifting the emotional tone. The core experiences include a point-and-click search game, a crafting minigame, a dress-up activity, and a final endless runner sequence. Each of these mini-games is tightly interwoven with the story, supported by a UI that evolves alongside the narrative arc.

The first three games are deliberately non-skill-based and low-stakes. The point-and-click and crafting sections are intuitive, simple enough for a child to complete, and emphasize exploration over challenge. The dress-up game serves as an aesthetic continuation, inviting the player to immerse themselves in the pastel, ultra-feminine visual world. The UI during these moments is clean, playful, and minimal, reinforcing a sense of harmless fun and comforting routine

However, the final endless runner game is different. The player takes on the role of the idol, running from a deranged fan wielding a knife. With each failed attempt, the fan gets closer, creating a mounting sense of dread. Playtesters have described this segment as stressful and anxiety-inducing, which is precisely the intended effect. This UI transformation reflects the collapse of the idealized fantasy and forces the player to confront the darker reality beneath the game’s initially cute veneer.

Revisions

User testing for Idol Chase yielded mostly positive feedback. Many users praised the art style, originality, and the overall concept of the game. These responses that were encouraging and affirmed the direction of the project. During the final presentation, I didn’t receive many suggestions for revision, so I extended testing by sending the game to my sister, who shared it with colleagues at her workplace for additional perspectives.

One particularly insightful response came from a senior art director, who noted that the crafting segment felt underwhelming. They mentioned that the “hunt-and-click” mechanic felt overly linear and lacked a sense of reward or payoff. According to them, while the narrative was compelling and emotionally resonant, the gameplay, particularly the crafting, struggled to keep pace. They also acknowledged that crafting is a notoriously tricky mechanic to implement in a way that feels both satisfying and meaningful.

Reflecting on this feedback, I realized they were right. During initial user tests, several players had difficulty with the crafting section, but no one explicitly called it out as an issue. I had interpreted the struggle as part of the experience, but this critique reframed it as a potential design flaw rather than an intentional challenge. It’s clear now that this section could benefit from revision to better support player engagement and narrative flow.

Summary

One of my biggest takeaways from this project was realizing just how much organization game development requires. Before any coding could begin, I had to plan out the script, design and draw assets, and map out the narrative structure and scenes. What surprised me most was how non-linear the process felt. I often found myself jumping between tasks: redrawing assets, rewriting dialogue, or revisiting scene transitions even when I thought the game was nearly complete. It taught me that iteration isn’t a setback—it’s part of the process.

The most challenging aspect was working with the pixel art style. I committed to creating all assets at a small scale to maintain visual consistency, but that introduced technical hurdles. When scaling graphics through Phaser.JS, I found that the canvas was too small to display readable text, which led to the difficult decision of mixing Phaser’s rendering system with DOM elements for text. Balancing those two approaches created unexpected complications and required a lot of problem-solving.

Despite the challenges, the most rewarding part was watching everything come together into a cohesive, emotional experience. Seeing players genuinely smile, laugh, or pause during heavy moments made all the hard work feel worthwhile. There's something deeply fulfilling about creating an interactive story that not only entertains but also resonates, and that sense of connection is what makes game development so meaningful to me.
